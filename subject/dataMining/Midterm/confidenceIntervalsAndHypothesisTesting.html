PART 1 • SLIDES 2–6

Part 1: Confidence Intervals — Estimating Where the Truth Lives

Moving beyond fragile single-number guesses to honest, mathematically sound probability intervals.

Slide 3: The Problem — A Point Estimate Is a Lucky Guess

Imagine a food critic survey covers 5% of the restaurants in London and reports an average meal price of £22.50.

The Fragile Reality:

The probability that the true average meal price across all London restaurants is exactly £22.500000... is effectively zero. A single sample mean (a point estimate) is almost guaranteed to be wrong by at least a few pennies.

The Honest Solution:

Instead of giving a single number and pretending it is certain, state a range and attach a confidence level to it: "We are 95% confident the true London average meal sits between £20.80 and £24.20."

Slide 4: Confidence Level — How Sure Is Sure? (1 − α)

The confidence level is written mathematically as 1 − α, where α (alpha) is the significance level, bounded between 0 and 1:

Confidence Level (1 − α) Significance Level (α) Split Tail Probability (α / 2) Standard Z Critical Value (zα/2) Typical Use Case
90% (0.90) 0.10 0.05 1.645 Exploratory analysis, quick business surveys
95% (0.95) 0.05 0.025 1.960 The Academic & Industry Default Standard
99% (0.99) 0.01 0.005 2.576 (~2.58) Clinical trials, aerospace, high-stakes finance
⚠ Crucial Exam Distinction: What 95% Confidence Actually Means
It does NOT mean there is a 95% probability that the true population mean μ lies within this particular calculated interval. The true parameter μ is a fixed, unchanging constant — it does not jump around! Rather, it means: "If we were to repeat this sampling procedure 100 times and calculate 100 different confidence intervals, exactly 95 of those intervals would capture the true population mean μ, and 5 would miss."

Slide 5: The Shape of Every Interval — One Formula, Three Ingredients

Every confidence interval across all of statistics shares an identical underlying mathematical architecture:

Confidence Interval = Point Estimate ± Reliability Factor × Standard Error
CI = [ Point Estimate − Margin of Error , Point Estimate + Margin of Error ]
  • Point Estimate: The best single guess computed directly from the sample (e.g., sample mean x̄ or difference in sample means x̄₁ − x̄₂).
  • Reliability Factor: A critical multiplier chosen from a theoretical probability distribution (Z or Student's T) matching the chosen confidence level (1 − α).
  • Standard Error (SE): The standard deviation of the sampling distribution, measuring how much sample estimates bounce around from sample to sample.

Slide 6: Two Fundamental Situations — Is Population Variance Known?

Situation A: Variance Known (σ is known)

When the true population standard deviation σ is known from historic census data or long-term production logs, we use the Standard Normal Z-Distribution.

Reliability Factor = zα/2  |  SE = σ / √n

Situation B: Variance Unknown (σ is unknown)

When population variance is unknown (almost all real-world data science!), we must estimate σ using sample standard deviation s. This introduces extra uncertainty, requiring Student's T-Distribution with degrees of freedom df = n − 1.

Reliability Factor = tn−1, α/2  |  SE = s / √n
CASE 1 • SLIDES 7–11

Case 1: One Population Mean — Population Variance Known

x̄ ± zα/2 × (σ ÷ √n)

Standard Error: SE = σ / √n  •  Margin of Error: ME = zα/2 × (σ / √n)

Slide 9: Worked Example — 95% Confidence Interval for Data Scientist Salaries

A staffing agency draws a random sample of n = 30 data scientists. The sample mean salary is x̄ = $100,200. Historical market records show a known population standard deviation of σ = $15,000. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the true mean salary μ.

Step-by-Step Derivation:
1. Identify Given Parameters: n = 30, x̄ = 100,200, σ = 15,000, 1 − α = 0.95 → α = 0.05, α/2 = 0.025.
2. Reliability Factor: From the Standard Normal Table, z0.025 = 1.96.
3. Standard Error: SE = σ / √n = 15,000 / √30 = 15,000 / 5.4772 = $2,738.61 (~$2,739).
4. Margin of Error: ME = 1.96 × 2,738.61 = $5,367.68 (~$5,368).
5. Confidence Interval Boundaries:
  • Lower Limit = 100,200 − 5,368 = $94,832
  • Upper Limit = 100,200 + 5,368 = $105,568
6. Sentence Conclusion: "We are 95% confident that the true mean annual salary of all data scientists in the target market lies between $94,832 and $105,568."

Slide 10: The Trade-off — More Confidence Costs Precision

What happens if the CEO demands 99% confidence instead of 95%?

95% Confidence (z = 1.96):
ME = 1.96 × 2,739 = $5,368
Interval: [$94,832, $105,568]
Total Span (Width): $10,736
99% Confidence (z = 2.58):
ME = 2.58 × 2,739 = $7,067
Interval: [$93,133, $107,267]
Total Span (Width): $14,134 (31.6% wider!)
💡 Slide 11 Intuition Check: How Wide Is Too Wide?
Suppose you are asked to estimate the age of a university student enrolled in an online course with no other information available:
• "Between 0 and 120 years old" → 100% confidence, but completely useless.
• "Between 21.4 and 21.6 years old" → Extremely precise, but almost certainly wrong (low confidence).
A confidence interval is an explicit balancing act between certainty (confidence level) and usefulness (precision/width).
STUDENT'S T • SLIDES 12–16

Student's T-Distribution & Case 2: Population Variance Unknown

Slide 12: A Short Story — The Statistician Who Wasn't Allowed a Name

In 1899, William Sealy Gosset was hired as an experimental brewer and chemist at the Guinness Brewery in Dublin, Ireland. Gosset faced a serious operational headache: Guinness wanted to evaluate the quality of barley yields, but testing large batches was expensive and destroyed the beer. Gosset had to work with tiny samples — often as small as n = 3 or 5.

When Gosset calculated standard Z-scores on these tiny samples, the intervals were systematically too narrow, and real errors occurred far more frequently than the theoretical 5%. Because Guinness strictly forbade its employees from publishing any scientific papers (to protect industrial trade secrets), Gosset published his discovery in 1908 in Biometrika under the humble pen name "Student". It was later championed and mathematically generalized by Ronald A. Fisher as the legendary Student's T-Distribution.

Slide 13: Same Bell Shape, Fatter Tails

Student's T distribution is symmetrical and bell-shaped like the Standard Normal distribution, but it has heavier, fatter tails. Why? Because we do not know σ, so we substitute s. Since sample standard deviation s fluctuates from sample to sample, we have two sources of randomness: x̄ in the numerator and s in the denominator.

  • Degrees of Freedom (df = n − 1): The shape of Student's T depends entirely on its degrees of freedom. With smaller df, the tails are fatter, pushing critical values further out (e.g., for 95% confidence with df=1, t = 12.706; with df=10, t = 2.228).
  • Convergence Rule of Thumb: As sample size grows past n > 50 (or df > 30), the T-distribution smoothly converges into the Standard Normal Z-distribution (at df=∞, t0.025 = 1.960).

Slides 14–15: Case 2 Worked Example — Nine Salaries Scraped from Glassdoor

A researcher scrapes n = 9 data scientist salaries from Glassdoor. The population standard deviation is completely unknown. The sample yield is:
• Sample Mean: x̄ = $92,533
• Sample Standard Deviation: s = $13,932
Construct a 95% confidence interval for the population mean salary μ.

Step-by-Step Derivation:
1. Identify Given Parameters: n = 9, x̄ = 92,533, s = 13,932, df = 9 − 1 = 8.
2. Reliability Factor: In the Student's T table, for df = 8 and tail area α/2 = 0.025 → t8, 0.025 = 2.306 (or 2.31).
3. Standard Error: SE = s / √n = 13,932 / √9 = 13,932 / 3 = $4,644.00.
4. Margin of Error: ME = 2.306 × 4,644 = $10,709.06 (using 2.31 gives $10,728).
5. Confidence Interval Boundaries:
  • Lower Limit = 92,533 − 10,728 = $81,805
  • Upper Limit = 92,533 + 10,728 = $103,261
6. Sentence Conclusion: "We are 95% confident that the true mean data scientist salary lies between $81,805 and $103,261."

Slide 16: Side by Side — The Severe Cost of Not Knowing Variance

Metric / Factor Case 1: Variance Known (σ known) Case 2: Variance Unknown (s used) Penalty / Consequence
Sample Size (n) n = 30 n = 9 √n drops from 5.48 to 3.00 (SE jumps)
Reliability Factor z = 1.960 t = 2.306 Multiplier increases by +17.7%

MARGIN OF ERROR • SLIDES 17–18

Margin of Error & The Three Levers

The entire span of a confidence interval is determined by the piece that follows the ± sign: the Margin of Error (ME):

ME = Reliability Factor × Standard Error = zα/2 × (σ / √n)  or  tdf, α/2 × (s / √n)

Slide 18: The Three Levers — What Actually Controls Interval Width

Lever 1: Confidence Level (1 − α)

• Increase confidence (e.g. 95% → 99%) → Reliability factor jumps (1.96 → 2.58) → ME widens (loss of precision).
• Decrease confidence (95% → 90%) → Reliability factor drops (1.96 → 1.645) → ME narrows.

Lever 2: Standard Deviation (σ or s)

• Inherent natural noise in the population.
• Higher variability in raw data → Larger SE → ME widens.
• As an analyst, you usually cannot manipulate population variability — it is a property of nature!

Lever 3: Sample Size (n) — The Engineer's Lever

• The only lever directly under the researcher's control!
• Increase n → √n grows → SE shrinks → ME narrows dramatically.
• Square Root Law: Because SE has √n in the denominator, cutting interval width in half requires collecting 4× as many samples.

TWO POPULATIONS • SLIDES 19–26

Confidence Intervals for Two Populations

When comparing two treatments, two demographics, or two branches, the first question is always: Are the samples dependent or independent?

Slides 20–21: Dependent Samples (Paired Differences) — The Magnesium Pill Study

A pharmaceutical lab measures blood magnesium levels (mg/dL) in n = 10 patients before and after taking a newly formulated pill.
Because the same 10 individuals are measured twice, the samples are paired (dependent). We reduce the two sets of numbers to a single column of differences: di = Afteri − Beforei.

Data Summary: n = 10, mean difference d̄ = 0.33 mg/dL, standard deviation of differences sd = 0.45 mg/dL, df = 10 − 1 = 9.
1. Reliability Factor: For 95% confidence and df = 9 → t9, 0.025 = 2.262.
2. Standard Error: SE = sd / √n = 0.45 / √10 = 0.45 / 3.1623 = 0.1423 mg/dL.
3. Margin of Error: ME = 2.262 × 0.1423 = 0.3219 mg/dL (~0.32 mg/dL).
4. Confidence Interval:
  • Lower Limit = 0.33 − 0.32 = +0.01 mg/dL (exact: 0.008 mg/dL)
  • Upper Limit = 0.33 + 0.32 = +0.65 mg/dL (exact: 0.652 mg/dL)
5. Clinical & Statistical Decision: "The 95% confidence interval [0.01, 0.65] is strictly positive throughout — zero is NOT contained in the interval. We conclude with 95% confidence that the magnesium pill produces a real, positive increase in blood magnesium levels."

Slides 22–23: Independent Samples Case 1 — Variances Known (UK University Grades)

A university assesses final exam percentage grades across two distinct departments:
• Department 1 (Engineering): n₁ = 100, x̄₁ = 58, known σ₁ = 10
• Department 2 (Management): n₂ = 70, x̄₂ = 65, known σ₂ = 12
Compute a 95% confidence interval for the difference between engineering and management scores (μ₁ − μ₂).

Step-by-Step Derivation:
1. Difference in Sample Means: x̄₁ − x̄₂ = 58 − 65 = −7.00 points.
2. Standard Error of Difference:
    SE = √[ (σ₁² / n₁) + (σ₂² / n₂) ] = √[ (100 / 100) + (144 / 70) ] = √[ 1.00 + 2.0571 ] = √3.0571 = 1.7485 points.
3. Margin of Error (z = 1.96): ME = 1.96 × 1.7485 = 3.427 points.
4. Interval Boundaries: −7.00 ± 3.43 → [−10.43, −3.57 points] (or [−9.28, −4.72] under standardized 99% or adjusted scale).
5. Interpretation: "Because both interval bounds are entirely negative, we are 95% confident that Engineering students score on average 3.57 to 10.43 points lower than Management students."

Slides 24–25: Independent Samples Case 2 — Variances Unknown, Assumed Equal (Apple Prices NY vs LA)

Are organic apples significantly more expensive in New York City than in Los Angeles?
• New York (Sample 1): n₁ = 10 grocery shops, x̄₁ = $3.80/lb, s₁ = $0.80/lb
• Los Angeles (Sample 2): n₂ = 8 grocery shops, x̄₂ = $2.60/lb, s₂ = $0.70/lb
Because variance is unknown but market dispersion is assumed equal (σ₁² = σ₂²), we calculate the Pooled Variance (sp²) with degrees of freedom df = n₁ + n₂ − 2 = 16:

Step-by-Step Derivation:
1. Pooled Sample Variance (sp²):
    sp² = [ (n₁ − 1)s₁² + (n₂ − 1)s₂² ] / [ n₁ + n₂ − 2 ]
    sp² = [ (9 × 0.64) + (7 × 0.49) ] / [ 10 + 8 − 2 ] = [ 5.76 + 3.43 ] / 16 = 9.19 / 16 = 0.5744 ($/lb)² (slide uses ~0.589).
2. Pooled Standard Deviation: sp = √0.5744 = 0.7579 $/lb.
3. Standard Error of Difference:
    SE = sp × √[ (1/n₁) + (1/n₂) ] = 0.7579 × √[ (1/10) + (1/8) ] = 0.7579 × √0.225 = 0.7579 × 0.4743 = $0.3595/lb (~$0.364).
4. Reliability Factor: For df = 16 at 95% confidence → t16, 0.025 = 2.120.
5. Margin of Error: ME = 2.120 × 0.364 = $0.772/lb.
6. Confidence Interval: (3.80 − 2.60) ± 0.772 = 1.20 ± 0.772 → [$0.43, $1.97/lb] (slide result: [$0.47, $1.93]).
7. Economic Conclusion: "The entire interval is strictly positive and bounded well above zero. We conclude with 95% confidence that organic apples are on average between $0.43 and $1.97 per pound more expensive in New York than in LA."

Slide 26: Independent Samples Case 3 — Variances Unknown and Assumed Different (Welch's Approximation)

When populations cannot be assumed to share equal variance (σ₁² ≠ σ₂²), pooling variances is invalid. We use separate sample variances in the standard error: SE = √[ (s₁² / n₁) + (s₂² / n₂) ]. The degrees of freedom are calculated using the Welch-Satterthwaite approximation, which generally yields a fractional df that is rounded down to the nearest integer.

PRACTICAL CASE STUDY • SLIDES 28–32

Al Bundy Shoe Shop — Data-Driven Retail Inventory Management

How can confidence intervals solve a multi-thousand dollar retail inventory bottleneck?

Step 1: The Problem & Data Inspection (Slides 28–29)
A shoe shop manager must order inventory for the upcoming season. If he stocks too few pairs, customers leave empty-handed (lost sales); if he stocks too many, excess capital is locked in unsold shoes. Foot sizes range from US Men's size 6.0 to 14.0 across 17 distinct half-sizes.

Step 2: The 17 Independent Confidence Intervals (Slide 30)
Using 12 months of historical sales data, the manager computes sample mean and standard deviation for every size. Because each shoe size represents a separate random variable, he constructs 17 separate 95% confidence intervals.
Stocking Rule: The manager orders the Upper Bound of each confidence interval! This guarantees with 95% mathematical confidence that customer demand for that specific size will be completely fulfilled.

Step 3: Comparing Two German Branches (GER1 vs GER2) (Slide 31)
Next, corporate asks: "Are women's shoe sales identical between store GER1 and store GER2?"
The manager computes the 95% confidence interval for the difference between women's shoe sales across both stores: [−1.24, +1.86 pairs].

Slide 32 The Insight: “No Result” Is Still a Powerful Result!
Because the interval spans from negative (−1.24) to positive (+1.86), zero is squarely contained inside the interval. This proves there is no statistically significant difference in customer preferences between the two German cities — corporate can use an identical merchandising strategy for both!
PART 2 • SLIDES 33–42

Part 2: Hypothesis Testing — When the Situation Calls for an Answer, Not a Range

Deciding yes or no: The 4-step framework, null vs alternative, significance α, critical regions, errors, and power.

Slide 34: Why Testing? Estimation Is Not a Decision

Confidence intervals tell you where a parameter is likely to be. But practical operations require action: Should we launch this drug? Is the machine out of calibration? Did the redesign increase conversion? Action requires a definite binary decision: Reject or Fail to Reject.

Slides 35–36: Step 1 — Formulating the Null (H₀) and Alternative (H₁)

H₀: The Null Hypothesis (Status Quo)

• Represents the default, boring assumption that nothing has changed, there is no effect, or the parameter equals a claimed baseline (μ = μ₀).
• Strict Exam Rule: The mathematical equality sign (=, ≤, ≥) ALWAYS resides in the Null Hypothesis!
• Analogy: Presumption of Innocence in a court of law ("The defendant is innocent").

H₁ (or Ha): The Alternative Hypothesis (Challenger)

• Represents what the researcher hopes to prove or detect: a real difference, a performance boost, or a defect (μ ≠ μ₀, μ > μ₀, or μ < μ₀).
• Bears the burden of proof.
• Analogy: The prosecution proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

Slide 37: Two Flavours of Test — One-Sided vs Two-Sided

Test Flavour Alternative Hypothesis Rejection Region Critical Value (α = 0.05) When to Use
Two-Sided (Two-Tailed) H₁: μ ≠ μ₀ α is split: α/2 in left tail, α/2 in right tail z = ±1.96 Testing if a machine is miscalibrated (too much or too little)
One-Sided Left-Tailed H₁: μ < μ₀ Entire α placed in far left tail z = −1.645 Testing if a diet reduces cholesterol
One-Sided Right-Tailed H₁: μ > μ₀ Entire α placed in far right tail z = +1.645 Testing if training boosts test scores

💡 Slide 42 Everyday Analogy: The University Dating Dilemma
A student is unsure whether a classmate returns their romantic interest:
• H₀: The classmate is NOT interested (status quo).
• H₁: The classmate IS interested.
• Type I Error (False Alarm): You conclude they like you and ask them out, but they reject you (α → embarrassment).
• Type II Error (Missed Opportunity): You assume they don't like you and do nothing, but they actually liked you (β → regret).
THE 5 TESTS • SLIDES 43–54

The Five Core Hypothesis Tests

Test 1: One Mean, Population Variance Known (Slides 43–44)

Glassdoor advertises that the average annual salary of data scientists is μ₀ = $113,000. A research firm surveys n = 30 data scientists, finding x̄ = $100,200 with known σ = $15,000. Test the claim at α = 0.05.

1. Hypotheses: H₀: μ = 113,000  vs  H₁: μ ≠ 113,000 (Two-sided).
2. Standard Error: SE = 15,000 / √30 = $2,738.61.
3. Test Statistic Z:
    Z = (x̄ − μ₀) / SE = (100,200 − 113,000) / 2,738.61 = −12,800 / 2,738.61 = −4.67.
4. Critical Values: At α = 0.05, critical bounds are ±1.96.
5. Decision: Because |−4.67| = 4.67 > 1.96, the test statistic falls deep in the rejection zone.
6. Conclusion: "We comfortably reject H₀ at the 5% significance level (p < 0.00001). There is overwhelming evidence that true average data scientist salaries are significantly different from Glassdoor's $113,000 claim."

Slides 45–46: The p-Value — The Universal Decision Benchmark

Formal Academic Definition:
The p-value is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis H₀ is true, of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the one actually observed. It represents the smallest significance level α at which H₀ can still be rejected.
Universal Decision Rule: If p-value < α → Reject H₀!
Why the p-Value Wins: You do not need to memorize or carry critical tables for every single distribution (Z, T, F, χ²). The p-value compresses all distributional complexity into a single number between 0 and 1.

Test 2: One Mean, Population Variance Unknown (Slides 47–48)

A company's email marketing open rate is historically 40% (μ₀ = 40.0). They examine n = 10 email campaigns from a top competitor, obtaining:
• Sample Mean: x̄ = 37.4%
• Sample Standard Deviation: s = 15.54%
Is the competitor performing differently from 40%? Test at α = 0.05.

1. Hypotheses: H₀: μ = 40.0  vs  H₁: μ ≠ 40.0 (Two-sided, df = 9).
2. Standard Error: SE = s / √n = 15.54 / √10 = 15.54 / 3.1623 = 4.914%.
3. Test Statistic T: T = (37.4 − 40.0) / 4.914 = −2.60 / 4.914 = −0.53.
4. p-Value Lookup: For df = 9 and |T| = 0.53 → p-value = 0.304.
5. Decision: Because p-value = 0.304 > 0.05, we fail to reject H₀.
6. Conclusion: "There is insufficient statistical evidence to conclude that the competitor's open rate differs from 40%."

Test 3: Two Means, Dependent Samples — Magnesium Pill Test (Slides 49–50)

Testing whether the magnesium pill raises blood magnesium: H₀: μd = 0  vs  H₁: μd > 0 (Right-tailed, n = 10, d̄ = 0.33, sd = 0.45, df = 9).

1. Test Statistic: T = (0.33 − 0) / (0.45 / √10) = 0.33 / 0.1423 = +2.29.
2. p-Value: For df = 9, one-tailed p-value = 0.024.
3. Slide 50 Insight — “It Depends Who Is Asking”:
  • At standard α = 0.05 → p = 0.024 < 0.05 → REJECT H₀ (The drug works!).
  • At strict α = 0.01 → p = 0.024 > 0.01 → FAIL TO REJECT H₀ (Insufficient clinical proof!).

Test 4: Two Means, Independent, Known Variances — Dean's Claim (Slides 51–52)

The Dean claims management students outperform engineering students by exactly 4 points: D₀ = 4.0.
Using our previous data (x̄₁ = 58, x̄₂ = 65, SE = 1.7485), test H₀: μ₁ − μ₂ = −4.0  vs  H₁: μ₁ − μ₂ ≠ −4.0.

1. Test Statistic Z:
    Z = [ (x̄₁ − x̄₂) − D₀ ] / SE = [ −7.0 − (−4.0) ] / 1.7485 = −3.0 / 1.7485 = −2.44.
2. p-Value: Two-tailed p-value for |Z| = 2.44 is p = 0.015.
3. Decision: At α = 0.05, p = 0.015 < 0.05 → Reject H₀.
4. Slide 52 Sign Trick: A two-sided test only tells you the Dean's claim of 4 points was wrong. The negative sign of Z (−2.44) tells you the direction: Management outperformed Engineering by even more than the Dean claimed!

Test 5: Two Means, Independent, Unknown Equal Variances — Apple Prices (Slides 53–54)

Testing H₀: μ₁ − μ₂ = 0  vs  H₁: μ₁ − μ₂ ≠ 0 (NY vs LA, x̄₁ − x̄₂ = $1.20, SEp = 0.364, df = 16).

1. Test Statistic T: T = (1.20 − 0) / 0.364 = +6.53.
2. p-Value: p ≈ 0.000001 (massive statistical significance).
3. Slide 54 Honest Caveat: A wildly significant result (p < 10−6) may simply prove an obvious, trivial question. Always distinguish between statistical significance (rejection of null) and practical/economic importance (is the effect size meaningful?).
REAL-WORLD INVESTIGATION • SLIDES 56–59

Spark Fortress Inc. — Testing the Gender Pay Gap

A masterclass in why data scientists must look past naive aggregate p-values:

Step 1: The Whole-Sample Naive Test (Slide 57)
A tech corporation with 98 male employees (x̄ = $76,400) and 76 female employees (x̄ = $72,800) is investigated for gender wage disparity. A pooled two-sample T-test yields:
• T = +1.34  |  df = 172  |  p-value = 0.182.
Naive Conclusion: Because p = 0.182 > 0.05, corporate lawyers declare there is no statistically significant pay gap.

Step 2: Inspecting the Raw Data by Hand (Slide 58)
When a data scientist plots the salary histogram, she spots a massive positive outlier in the female group: the President & CEO, Caroline Bold, earns $450,000! This single extreme salary artificially pulled up the entire female sample mean and inflated female standard deviation.

Step 3: Age & Seniority Segmentation (Slide 59)
Segmenting employees into proper peer groups reveals the hidden truth:
• Junior Cohort (< 35 years old): Men earn on average $4,200 more (p = 0.012 < 0.05 → Statistically Significant Gap!).
• Senior Cohort (> 35 years old): Men earn on average $7,800 more (p = 0.004 < 0.05 → Statistically Significant Gap!).
Core Takeaway: Aggregation can completely mask real effects (Simpson's Paradox). Always inspect distributions and segment data meaningfully before trusting a high-level test.
SUMMARY • SLIDES 61–62

Seven Fundamental Things to Carry Forward

  1. A point estimate is a guess; an interval is an honest answer. Always report uncertainty bounds.
  2. Standard error measures the precision of the sample mean (σ / √n), shrinking with the square root of n.
  3. Student's T accounts for the extra uncertainty of having to estimate σ with sample standard deviation s.
  4. Degrees of freedom dictate tail thickness — smaller samples demand wider margins of error.
  5. The equality sign (=, ≤, ≥) ALWAYS belongs in the Null Hypothesis (H₀).
  6. A p-value measures evidence against H₀ — smaller p-values indicate stronger evidence to reject the status quo.
  7. Statistical significance does not equal practical importance — always look at the magnitude and business context of the effect size.

📖 Pillar 1: Core Terminology & Keyword Recall Grid

Term / Concept Symbol Formal Definition High-Yield Exam Context
Confidence Interval CI A numerical range of plausible values for an unknown population parameter with an associated probability. Centered at the point estimate (x̄); width equals 2 × ME.
Margin of Error ME The maximum expected difference between the true population parameter and sample estimate at a given confidence level. ME = Reliability Factor × Standard Error.
Confidence Level 1 − α The long-run proportion of repeated confidence intervals that will enclose the true population parameter. Standard is 95% (α = 0.05). Higher confidence widens ME.
Degrees of Freedom df The number of independent pieces of information that go into estimating a parameter. Single sample: df = n − 1; Two independent pooled: df = n₁ + n₂ − 2.
Student's T-Distribution t A symmetric, bell-shaped distribution with fatter tails than Z, used when population variance is unknown. Discovered by William Gosset at Guinness. Approaches Z as n > 50.
Pooled Sample Variance sp² A weighted average of two sample variances used when two independent populations have equal unknown variances. Weights each sample variance by its degrees of freedom (n − 1).
Null Hypothesis H₀ The baseline proposition that no effect, no difference, or no relationship exists in the population. Always contains the equality sign (=, ≤, or ≥).
Alternative Hypothesis H₁ (Ha) The research claim that a parameter differs from H₀ (≠, >, or <). Dictates whether the test is two-tailed (≠) or one-tailed (>, <).
Significance Level α The maximum tolerated probability of making a Type I error (rejecting a true null hypothesis). Set before seeing data; commonly 0.05, 0.01, or 0.10.
p-Value p The probability of obtaining sample results as extreme as observed, assuming H₀ is true. Decision rule: If p < α → Reject H₀. Smaller p = stronger evidence.
Type I Error α Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true (False Positive). Controlled directly by setting α. Convicting an innocent person.
Type II Error β Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is actually false (False Negative). Power of the test equals 1 − β. Letting a guilty person go free.

🔧 Pillar 2: Formula & Calculation Reference Cards

All formula cards contain explicit mathematical formulations, variable breakdowns, and plain English verbalizations.

Single Mean CI (σ Known)
x̄ ± zα/2 × (σ ÷ √n)
Parameter Definitions:
  • x̄: Sample mean (point estimate).
  • zα/2: Standard normal critical value for confidence level 1 − α.
  • σ: Known population standard deviation.
  • n: Sample size drawn from the population.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Sample mean x-bar plus or minus critical z of alpha over two, multiplied by population standard deviation sigma divided by the square root of sample size n."
Single Mean CI (σ Unknown • Student's T)
x̄ ± tn−1, α/2 × (s ÷ √n)
Parameter Definitions:
  • s: Sample standard deviation (used because σ is unknown).
  • tn−1, α/2: Student's T critical value at df = n − 1.
  • df: Degrees of freedom (n − 1).
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Sample mean x-bar plus or minus critical t with n minus one degrees of freedom at alpha over two, multiplied by sample standard deviation s divided by the square root of n."
Paired Differences CI (Dependent Samples)
d̄ ± tn−1, α/2 × (sd ÷ √n)
Parameter Definitions:
  • d̄: Mean of the pairwise sample differences (di = x1,i − x2,i).
  • sd: Standard deviation of the pairwise differences.
  • n: Number of matched pairs.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Mean difference d-bar plus or minus critical t with n minus one degrees of freedom at alpha over two, multiplied by standard deviation of differences s-sub-d divided by the square root of n."
Difference of Two Means CI (σ₁, σ₂ Known)
(x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± zα/2 × √[ (σ₁² ÷ n₁) + (σ₂² ÷ n₂) ]
Parameter Definitions:
  • (x̄₁ − x̄₂): Difference between the two sample means.
  • σ₁², σ₂²: Known population variances of the two groups.
  • n₁, n₂: Respective sample sizes of both groups.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Difference of sample means x-bar one minus x-bar two, plus or minus critical z of alpha over two, multiplied by the square root of sigma one squared over n one plus sigma two squared over n two."
Pooled Variance (sp² for Equal Variances)
sp² = [ (n₁ − 1)s₁² + (n₂ − 1)s₂² ] ÷ (n₁ + n₂ − 2)
Parameter Definitions:
  • s₁², s₂²: Sample variances of the two independent groups.
  • df = n₁ + n₂ − 2: Combined degrees of freedom.
  • sp: Pooled standard deviation = √(sp²).
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Pooled variance s-p squared equals open parenthesis n one minus one close parenthesis times s one squared, plus open parenthesis n two minus one close parenthesis times s two squared, all divided by n one plus n two minus two."
Two Means CI (Equal Unknown Variances)
(x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± tdf, α/2 × sp √[ (1 ÷ n₁) + (1 ÷ n₂) ]
Parameter Definitions:
  • tdf, α/2: Student's T critical value at df = n₁ + n₂ − 2.
  • sp: Pooled standard deviation.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Difference of sample means x-bar one minus x-bar two, plus or minus critical t with df degrees of freedom at alpha over two, multiplied by pooled standard deviation s-p times the square root of one over n one plus one over n two."
One-Sample Z-Test Statistic
Z = (x̄ − μ₀) ÷ (σ ÷ √n)
Parameter Definitions:
  • μ₀: Hypothesized population mean stated in H₀.
  • σ / √n: Standard error of the mean under known σ.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Test statistic Z equals sample mean x-bar minus hypothesized population mean mu-naught, divided by population standard deviation sigma over the square root of sample size n."
One-Sample T-Test Statistic
T = (x̄ − μ₀) ÷ (s ÷ √n)
Parameter Definitions:
  • s / √n: Estimated standard error using sample standard deviation.
  • df: Degrees of freedom = n − 1.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Test statistic T equals sample mean x-bar minus hypothesized mean mu-naught, divided by sample standard deviation s over the square root of n, evaluated at n minus one degrees of freedom."
Paired Differences T-Test Statistic
T = (d̄ − D₀) ÷ (sd ÷ √n)
Parameter Definitions:
  • D₀: Hypothesized mean difference (usually 0).
  • sd / √n: Standard error of differences; df = n − 1.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Test statistic T equals sample mean difference d-bar minus hypothesized difference D-naught, divided by standard deviation of differences s-sub-d over the square root of n."
Two-Sample Pooled T-Test Statistic
T = [ (x̄₁ − x̄₂) − D₀ ] ÷ [ sp × √((1/n₁) + (1/n₂)) ]
Parameter Definitions:
  • D₀: Hypothesized difference between population means (μ₁ − μ₂).
  • df = n₁ + n₂ − 2: Pooled degrees of freedom.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Test statistic T equals difference of sample means x-bar one minus x-bar two minus hypothesized difference D-naught, divided by pooled standard deviation s-p times the square root of one over n one plus one over n two."

Dimension Confidence Intervals (Estimation) Hypothesis Testing (Decision)

Property Standard Normal (Z) Student's T-Distribution
Variance Requirement Population σ must be KNOWN Population σ is UNKNOWN (s is used)

⚠️ Pillar 4: Common Exam Pitfalls & Fallacies

Pitfall 1: Misinterpreting the 95% Confidence Interval

Fatal Mistake: Saying "There is a 95% probability that μ lies between 94,832 and 105,568."
Correct Understanding: The true population parameter μ is a constant; it is either inside this specific interval or it is not. The 95% refers to the reliability of the estimation procedure across repeated random samples.

Pitfall 2: Putting Hypothesized Claims in H₀ with an Inequality

Fatal Mistake: Writing H₀: μ > 50 or H₀: μ ≠ 50.
Correct Understanding: In classical Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing, the equality condition (=, ≤, ≥) MUST always reside in the Null Hypothesis H₀. The research hypothesis with strict inequality (≠, >, <) always belongs in H₁.

Pitfall 3: Using Z Instead of T When Variance is Unknown

Fatal Mistake: Looking up 1.96 when given a sample standard deviation s = 13,932 and n = 9.
Correct Understanding: Whenever population variance σ is unknown, you MUST use Student's T distribution with df = n − 1. Using Z artificially compresses the interval and leads to severe exam point deductions.

Pitfall 4: Forgetting the Non-Zero Difference D₀

Fatal Mistake: Testing whether Management outscores Engineering by 4 points, but calculating Z = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / SE instead of [ (x̄₁ − x̄₂) − (−4) ] / SE.
Correct Understanding: When testing a specific non-zero gap claim, you must subtract D₀ from the sample difference!
