PART 1

Descriptive Statistics

Organizing, summarizing, and honestly describing the data in front of you.

  • Know your data: population or sample, type, and measurement level
  • Choose the right picture: tables, bars, pies, Pareto, histograms, scatter plots
  • Summarize with numbers: center, asymmetry, spread, and relationships

Population vs. Sample

The first question of every analysis: which one am I holding?

POPULATION (N)
  • All items of interest to the study
  • Numbers computed from it are parameters
  • Hard to define, hard to observe, costly
SAMPLE (n)
  • A subset drawn from the population
  • Numbers computed from it are statistics
  • Faster and cheaper - what you will almost always use
A thought experiment: "all students."

"All students of a university" includes students on campus, at home, abroad, on exchange, part-time, and even enrolled high schoolers and the list still misses someone. Populations are hard to pin down; statistical tests are designed for the incomplete data of samples.

What Makes a Sample Good?

Two conditions and a canteen that failed both.

1. RANDOM

Every member of the population is chosen strictly by chance — everyone has a shot at being selected.

2. REPRESENTATIVE

The subset accurately reflects the members of the entire population — not just one convenient corner of it.

Case: 50 students interviewed in the university canteen
  • Not random: only students at lunch could be picked; most members never had a chance.
  • Not representative: it reflects "students who eat at the canteen," not all students of the university.
  • The fix: random selection from the full student database - which usually needs institutional access.

Types of Data

Classify first the type of data decides which statistics and charts are even possible.

CATEGORICAL

  • Describes categories or groups
  • Car brands: Mercedes, BMW, Audi
  • Yes / No answers ("Do you own a car?")

NUMERICAL: DISCRETE

  • Countable, finite you can list every value
  • Number of children, scores, grades
  • Pizza prices in pesos: you can pay 199.25, not 199.253 the smallest step is 1 centavo

NUMERICAL: CONTINUOUS

  • Infinite, impossible to count
  • Weight, height, area, distance, time
  • A drop of sweat changes your true weight - the scale just can't see it

Levels of Measurement

Qualitative (nominal, ordinal) Quantitative (interval, ratio)

Level Nature Characteristics & Examples
NOMINAL Qualitative Categories with no order. Car brands; the four seasons.
ORDINAL Qualitative Categories with strict order. Lunch ratings: disgusting → unappetizing → neutral → tasty → delicious.
INTERVAL Quantitative Numbers without a true zero. °C and °F - 0° is an artificial, man-made point, so ratios are meaningless.
RATIO Quantitative Numbers with a true zero. Counts, distance, time and Kelvin: 0 K is where atoms stop moving.
Why temperature trips people up:

10°C vs. 5°C looks "twice as warm" - but the same days are 50°F vs. 41°F. No true zero, no ratios. In Kelvin, ratios are real.

❓ Knowledge Check 1

Think first, then check answers below.

Q1. You survey every 10th customer leaving a mall to study "shoppers in this city." Is this sample random? Is it representative?

Answer: Neither. It is not random because shoppers outside this specific mall never had a chance of selection; it is not representative because it reflects mall shoppers at one location/time, not all shoppers across the city.

Q2. SAT scores can only take specific listed values. Discrete or continuous? What about your exact height?

Answer: SAT scores are Discrete (finite, countable increments like 10-point steps). Your exact height is Continuous (infinite decimal possibilities along a continuous continuum).

Q3. Classify: jersey numbers of a basketball team, and temperature in Kelvin type and level of measurement for each.

Answer: Jersey numbers = Categorical, Nominal (numerical labels/names only; mean jersey number is meaningless). Temperature in Kelvin = Numerical Continuous, Ratio (absolute true zero where molecular motion stops).

Visualizing Categorical Data

Case: a shop selling German cars Audi, Mercedes, BMW (illustrative units sold).

  • Frequency distribution table: Category + frequency. The starting point for every chart.
  • Bar chart: One bar per category. Fastest way to compare groups.
  • Pie chart: Built on relative frequency (shares sum to 100%). The classic market-share view.
  • Pareto diagram: Bars in descending order + a cumulative-frequency curve to 100%.
Audi: 62
Mercedes: 50
BMW: 44
Sorted by frequency one cumulative line away from a Pareto diagram.

The Pareto Diagram & the 80-20 Rule

A bar chart, sorted - plus one powerful curve.

  • Order categories by descending frequency
  • Add a cumulative frequency curve the running sum of relative frequencies, ending at 100%
  • Read on a second axis (right side); each vertex shows the subtotal of everything to its left
  • Combines the strengths of the bar chart (comparison) and the pie chart (share of total)
  • Instantly answers: "What do the top 2 categories cover?"
Vilfredo Pareto's principle

80% of the effects come from 20% of the causes.
Example: Microsoft reported that fixing 20% of software bugs eliminated 80% of the problems customers experienced.

Grouping Numerical Data into Intervals

20 unique numbers = a useless 20-row table. Group first, then plot.

interval width = (largest - smallest) ÷ (number of intervals)
Interval Frequency Relative freq.
1–21 2 10%
21–41 4 20%
41–61 3 15%
61–81 6 30%
81–101 5 25%
Total 20 100%
  • Aim for 5-20 intervals, depending on data size
  • Example: (100 - 1) ÷ 5 = 19.8 → round up to 20 for neatness
  • A value joins an interval if it is > lower bound and ≤ upper bound
  • Relative frequency = interval frequency ÷ total observations

The Histogram

The most common graph for numerical data a bar chart's cousin, not its twin.

  • Both axes are numerical: intervals on X, frequency on Y
  • Bars touch - each interval ends where the next begins
  • Bar charts keep bars apart, because categories are separate things
  • Can plot relative frequency (%) instead same shape, different message
  • Unequal intervals exist (survey age brackets like 18-25 ... 60+), but stick to equal widths until you're experienced

Near-zero gaps between bars signal continuity between intervals.

Two Variables at Once

Cross tables for categories, scatter plots for numbers.

Cross (contingency) table
  • Two categorical variables: e.g., 3 investors × 3 asset classes (stocks, bonds, real estate)
  • Always add row & column totals rows give totals per asset class, columns give each investor's holdings
  • Visualize with a side-by-side bar chart to compare within or across investors
Scatter plot
  • Two numerical variables: 100 students' SAT reading (X) vs. writing (Y), each point one student
  • Read the pattern, not the points: clear uptrend; a dense cluster near the 500-average middle; top students excel at both
  • Jane: 550 reading, 300 writing an outlier that defies the data set's logic

❓ Knowledge Check 2

Think first, then check answers below.

Q1. Your bar chart of ice-cream flavors has bars touching each other. What's wrong, and why does the convention exist?

Answer: Touching bars falsely imply a continuous numerical continuum between categories. Bar charts for categorical data must keep bars separated to signal distinct categories.

Q2. A dataset ranges from 4 to 92 and you want 8 intervals. What interval width would you use?

Answer: Width = (92 - 4) / 8 = 88 / 8 = 11. (You could round up to 11 or 12 for clean interval boundaries).

Q3. In a scatter plot of study hours vs. exam scores, one student studied 40 hours and scored 35%. What do we call this point and how should we treat it for now?

Answer: An outlier. Investigate it first (check for data entry error or exceptional circumstances) before deciding whether to retain or remove it.

Mean, Median, Mode

One dataset, three answers NYC pizza prices, with one $66 posh outlier.

MEAN: $11

Sum ÷ count. μ for a population, x̄ for a sample. Fragile: one $66 pizza doubled it.

MEDIAN: $6

Middle of the ordered list, position (n+1)÷2. With an even n, average the two middle values. Immune to the outlier.

MODE: $3

The most frequent value. Works for categories too. No repeats → no mode; 2-3 modes are tolerable, more is noise.

There is no single "best" measure but relying on only one is definitely the worst.

Skewness Which Way Does the Tail Point?

The direction of the skew follows the TAIL (the outliers), not the bulk of the data.

Right (positive) skew
mean > median

Tail & outliers on the right.

Symmetrical (zero skew)
mean = median = mode

Perfectly centered on the mean.

Left (negative) skew
mean < median

Tail & outliers on the left.

The mode always marks the highest point. Skewness links central tendency to probability theory.

Variance - Dispersion Around the Mean

Why we square, and why the sample formula divides by n - 1.

Population Variance
σ² = Σ(x - μ)² / N
Sample Variance
s² = Σ(x - x̄)² / (n - 1)

Why square the differences?

  • Keeps results non-negative dispersion is distance, and distance can't be negative. Raw differences would cancel out to nothing.
  • Amplifies big deviations: mean 0, observation 100 → squared spread of 10,000.
Worked example: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}, mean = 3
  • As a population: σ² = 10 ÷ 5 = 2.0
  • As a sample: s² = 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5
  • The sample version corrects upward - the sample may come from a more spread-out population (e.g., one concentrated at 1s and 5s, with variance 2.96).

Standard Deviation & Coefficient of Variation

Same pizzas, two currencies and why we need a unitless measure.

Standard deviation = √(variance)
Back in the original units, so it's directly interpretable - "square dollars" mean nothing, even in statistics. The go-to measure of variability for a single dataset.
CV = standard deviation ÷ mean
"Relative standard deviation." Unitless which makes variability comparable across datasets and across units. Comparing raw standard deviations is meaningless.
Same 11 pizza prices, in dollars vs. Philippine pesos ($1 = ₱56):
  • $3.27 std. dev. in dollars
  • ₱183.12 std. dev. in pesos
  • 0.60 CV in dollars
  • 0.60 CV in pesos
Identical variability, finally visible.

Covariance & Correlation

Direction is easy. Strength needs standardizing.

Covariance - the direction
  • > 0: variables move together (house size & price)
  • < 0: they move in opposite directions
  • = 0: independent movements
  • Problem: no scale it can be 0.0023 or 30 million. Unreadable.
Correlation - the strength
r = covariance ÷ (std-dev X × std-dev Y)
  • Always between -1 and +1 no exceptions
  • House size vs. price: r = 0.87 → strong positive
  • Ice cream vs. umbrella sales: negative sun helps one, rain the other

Symmetry: corr(X, Y) = corr(Y, X). Size-and-price equals price-and-size the formula can't tell them apart.

Correlation ≠ causation: Size causes price a house that gets pricier doesn't grow bigger. Regression analysis digs into causality later.

❓ Knowledge Check 3

Think first, then check answers below.

Q1. A dataset of salaries has mean 45,000 and median 32,000. Which way is it skewed, and what likely causes it?

Answer: Right (positive) skew because mean > median. High-earning executive outliers pull the mean to the right.

Q2. Sample {2, 4, 6}: compute the sample variance. Why divide by 2 and not 3?

Answer: Mean = 4. Squared diffs: (2-4)²=4, (4-4)²=0, (6-4)²=4 → sum = 8. Dividing by n - 1 = 2 gives sample variance = 4. We divide by n - 1 (Bessel's correction) to prevent systematically underestimating population variance.

Q3. Dataset A: mean 20, std. dev. 4. Dataset B: mean 400, std. dev. 40. Which is more variable, and which measure told you?

Answer: Dataset A is more variable! CV(A) = 4 / 20 = 0.20 (20%). CV(B) = 40 / 400 = 0.10 (10%). The Coefficient of Variation (CV) tells you relative variability regardless of raw scale.

PART 2

Inferential Statistics

Using probability theory and distributions to predict population values from sample data.

  • Distributions: what they are and the one that rules them all
  • The normal distribution, standardization, and the Central Limit Theorem
  • Standard error, estimators, and point estimates the road to confidence intervals

What Is a Distribution?

A function showing every possible value of a variable and how often each occurs.

  • One fair die: each face has probability 1/6 (≈ 0.17); a seven has probability 0
  • A distribution covers ALL possible values complete when probabilities sum to 1 (100%)
  • Equal chances for every outcome = the discrete uniform distribution
  • The graph is only a picture of the distribution the distribution IS the underlying probabilities
  • Two dice: 36 combinations; guessing the sum is a game of unequal chances → Seven is king: 6/36 ≈ 0.17

So far: discrete examples. Inference mostly runs on continuous distributions - starting with the normal.

The Normal (Gaussian) Distribution

The bell curve the most common distribution in statistics, denoted N ∼ (μ, σ²).

mean = median = mode
  • Perfectly symmetrical, zero skew, centered on the mean
  • Highest point at the mean which coincides with the mode
  • Change the mean (fixed σ): the curve slides left or right, shape intact
  • Change σ (fixed mean): the curve reshapes in place:
    • lower σ → tall & tight, thinner tails
    • higher σ → flat & wide, fatter tails
  • Why we care: it approximates a huge range of variables, its statistics are elegant, and decisions built on it have a strong track record

Standardization & the Z-Score

Any distribution can be rescaled to mean 0, standard deviation 1.

z = (x - μ) ÷ σ
  • Standardizing a normal distribution gives the standard normal, denoted Z
  • Step 1: subtract μ from every point: the curve slides so its center is 0; shape (and σ) unchanged
  • Step 2: divide every point by σ: the curve reshapes so its spread is exactly 1
  • Result: predictions and inference get dramatically easier
Worked example: mean 3, σ = 1.22
  • Original: 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5
  • - mean (3): -2, -1, -1, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 2
  • ÷ σ (1.22): -1.63, -0.82, -0.82, 0, 0, 0, 0.82, 0.82, 1.63

New mean = 0, new σ = 1 - and note: adding or subtracting a constant never changes the standard deviation.

Sampling Distributions

The used-car problem: every sample tells you a slightly different story.

Sample mean: $2,617
Sample mean: $3,201
Sample mean: $2,844
  • One sample mean is a single, shaky approximation of the population mean
  • So draw many samples and collect their means into a new dataset
  • That dataset's distribution is the sampling distribution of the mean
  • The means cluster around one value the population mean itself (≈ $2,800 here)
  • Averaging the sample means gives a very precise approximation of μ

The Central Limit Theorem

The discovery that revolutionized statistics.

No matter the shape of the population skewed, uniform, exponential, anything the sampling distribution of the mean approximates a normal distribution.
  • μ: the sampling distribution's mean equals the population mean
  • σ² / n: its variance is the population variance over the sample size bigger samples, tighter results
  • n ≥ 30: the usual rule of thumb for the CLT to apply

Why it matters: we can run tests, build confidence intervals, and make inferences with the normal distribution even when the population itself is nothing like normal. The car prices were right-skewed; their sample means were normal anyway.

Standard Error

The standard deviation of the sampling distribution variability of the sample means.

SE = σ ÷ √n
Feel it with numbers (σ = 100):
  • n = 25 → SE = 20
  • n = 100 → SE = 10
  • n = 400 → SE = 5
  • Comes straight from the CLT: variance σ²/n → take the square root
  • Tells you how well your sample mean approximates the true mean
  • Appears in almost every statistical test that's why it earned its own name
  • Shrinks as n grows: bigger samples → better approximations of the population

Estimators & Point Estimates

"Estimators are like judges we want them unbiased and efficient."

The vocabulary
  • Estimator: a rule for approximating a population parameter from sample data alone
  • Point estimate: a single number (x̄ estimates μ; s² estimates σ²)
  • It sits exactly at the center of a confidence interval which carries more information
The two properties
  • Unbiased: expected value equals the true parameter
  • Efficient: among unbiased estimators, the one with the smallest variance
  • "Statistic" is the broader term every point estimate is a statistic
The "plus one foot" estimator:

"Estimate the average American height as the sample mean plus one foot." It is an estimator just a biased one, with a bias of exactly one foot. The sensible, unbiased estimator is the sample mean itself.

❓ Knowledge Check 4

Think first, then check answers below.

Q1. A population of incomes is heavily right-skewed. You repeatedly draw samples of n = 50 and record each mean. What shape does the distribution of those means take, and why?

Answer: Approximately Normal (bell-shaped). By the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), because n ≥ 30 (n = 50), the sampling distribution of the sample mean is normal regardless of the underlying population skew.

Q2. σ = 60 and n = 36. Compute the standard error. What happens to it if n rises to 144?

Answer: SE = σ / √n = 60 / √36 = 60 / 6 = 10. If n rises to 144, SE = 60 / √144 = 60 / 12 = 5. Quadrupling sample size cuts the standard error in half!

Q3. A classmate proposes estimating average commute time as "sample mean minus 10 minutes, to be safe." Evaluate this estimator using the two properties.

Answer: It is BIASED (expected value is μ - 10, not μ). While it may have the same variance as the sample mean, its bias makes it mathematically inferior to the unbiased sample mean x̄.

📌 Key Takeaways

If you remember six things from this unit, make it these.

  • Classify before you compute: population or sample, data type, measurement level. Everything downstream depends on it.
  • Match the chart to the data: bars/pies/Pareto for categories, histograms and scatter plots for numbers.
  • Mean, median, mode are a team: Outliers bend the mean; skew direction follows the tail.
  • Variance → standard deviation → CV: from squared units, to real units, to unit-free comparison.
  • The CLT lets sample means behave normally: even when the population doesn't with mean μ and standard error σ/√n.
  • Correlation caps at ± 1 and is symmetric: and it never, by itself, proves causation.

📐 Pillar 2: Formula & Calculation Reference Cards

Fundamental equations for descriptive grouping, dispersion, standardization, and sampling theory.

Histogram Interval Width
Width = (Largest - Smallest) ÷ k
Parameter Definitions:
  • Largest: Maximum numerical observation in the raw dataset.
  • Smallest: Minimum numerical observation in the raw dataset.
  • k: Target number of class intervals (typically 5 to 20).
Plain English Reading: "Interval width equals the range between highest and lowest values divided by the number of desired bins, rounded up for clean boundaries."
Worked Example: Range from 1 to 100 with 5 intervals → (100 - 1) / 5 = 19.8 → round up to 20.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Interval width equals largest observation minus smallest observation all divided by the target number of intervals k."
Sample Mean & Population Mean
x̄ = (Σ x_i) ÷ n  |  μ = (Σ X_i) ÷ N
Parameter Definitions:
  • Σ x_i: Sum of all individual observation values.
  • n: Sample size (total count of sample observations).
  • N: Population size (total count of all population members).
Plain English Reading: "Add up every value in the dataset and divide by the total number of observations to find the center of gravity."
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Sample mean x-bar equals summation of x sub-i divided by sample size n. Population mean mu equals summation of capital X sub-i divided by population size N."
Sample Variance & Standard Deviation
s² = Σ (x_i - x̄)² ÷ (n - 1)  |  s = √(s²)
Parameter Definitions:
  • (x_i - x̄)²: Squared deviation of each point from the sample mean.
  • (n - 1): Degrees of freedom (Bessel's correction for sample bias).
  • s: Sample standard deviation expressed in original units.
Plain English Reading: "Square each deviation from the mean, sum them, divide by sample size minus one for variance, and take the square root for standard deviation."
Worked Example: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}, mean 3 → sum of squares = 10 → s² = 10 / 4 = 2.5 → s = 1.58.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Sample variance s squared equals summation of open parenthesis x sub-i minus x-bar close parenthesis squared divided by n minus one. Standard deviation s equals the square root of variance."
Coefficient of Variation (CV)
CV = s ÷ x̄  |  CV% = (s ÷ x̄) × 100%
Parameter Definitions:
  • s: Standard deviation of the sample.
  • x̄: Arithmetic mean of the sample.
  • CV: Relative dispersion index (completely unitless).
Plain English Reading: "Divide standard deviation by the mean to get relative spread, enabling direct comparison across completely different units and scales."
Worked Example: Pizza in USD: $3.27 / $11 = 0.60. Pizza in PHP: ₱183.12 / ₱616 = 0.60. Identical relative variability!
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Coefficient of variation CV equals standard deviation s divided by sample mean x-bar, multiplied by one hundred percent."
Pearson Correlation Coefficient
r = Cov(X, Y) ÷ (s_X × s_Y)
Parameter Definitions:
  • Cov(X, Y): Sample covariance indicating direction of linear co-movement.
  • s_X, s_Y: Standard deviations of variables X and Y.
  • r: Correlation coefficient strictly bounded in [-1, +1].
Plain English Reading: "Standardize covariance by dividing by the product of both standard deviations to obtain a dimensionless strength score from -1 to +1."
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Correlation coefficient r equals covariance of X and Y divided by the product of standard deviation of X and standard deviation of Y."
Standard Score (Z-Score)
z = (x - μ) ÷ σ  |  z = (x - x̄) ÷ s
Parameter Definitions:
  • x: Individual raw observation value.
  • μ, x̄: Population or sample mean.
  • σ, s: Population or sample standard deviation.
  • z: Standard score indicating distances from the center in units of standard deviation.
Plain English Reading: "Subtract the mean from the raw score and divide by the standard deviation to convert the distribution to center zero and spread one."
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Z equals raw score x minus mean mu all divided by standard deviation sigma."
Standard Error of the Mean (SE)
SE = σ ÷ √n  |  Var(x̄) = σ² ÷ n
Parameter Definitions:
  • σ: Standard deviation of the parent population.
  • n: Sample size of drawn observations (n ≥ 30 for CLT).
  • SE: Standard error measuring the standard deviation of sample means.
Plain English Reading: "Standard error equals population standard deviation divided by square root of sample size; variance of the sample mean is population variance over n."
Worked Example: σ = 100, n = 100 → SE = 100 / √100 = 10. If n = 400 → SE = 100 / 20 = 5.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Standard error of the mean SE equals population standard deviation sigma divided by the square root of sample size n."

⚠️ Pillar 4: Common Exam Pitfalls & Fallacies

Crucial distinctions, traps, and misconceptions frequently tested on Statistics Midterm exams.

1. The "10°C is Twice as Warm as 5°C" Ratio Fallacy

The Trap: Calculating ratios on Celsius or Fahrenheit temperatures.
The Reality: °C and °F are Interval scales with an arbitrary zero (freezing point of water). In Fahrenheit, 10°C is 50°F and 5°C is 41°F (50 is not 2 × 41). Only Kelvin has a true natural zero (0 K = absolute cessation of atomic motion) allowing valid ratios.

2. Bar Chart vs. Histogram Visual Confusion

The Trap: Drawing bars that touch on a bar chart or leaving gaps on a histogram.
The Reality: Bar charts represent discrete categories and MUST have spaces between bars. Histograms represent continuous numerical intervals where bars MUST touch to signify continuity between adjacent class boundaries.

3. The Skewness "Hump vs. Tail" Misconception

The Trap: Stating that a distribution is right-skewed because the "hump" (bulk of data) is on the right.
The Reality: Skewness follows the TAIL and the outliers, not the hump! A right-skewed distribution has its hump on the left and its long tail stretching to the right (mean > median).

4. The Sample Variance Denominator Trap (Dividing by n)

The Trap: Computing sample variance by dividing sum of squared deviations by sample size n.
The Reality: You must divide by (n - 1) degrees of freedom (Bessel's correction). Dividing by n systematically underestimates the true population variance because sample observations cluster closer to their own sample mean x̄ than to the true population mean μ.

5. Correlation Implies Causation Fallacy

The Trap: Claiming that a high correlation (e.g., r = 0.87) proves that variable X caused variable Y.
The Reality: Correlation is completely symmetric (corr(X, Y) = corr(Y, X)) and only proves linear association. It cannot distinguish which variable drove the other, nor can it rule out lurking third variables.

6. Standard Deviation vs. Standard Error Confusion

The Trap: Using standard deviation when asked for the precision of an estimated mean.
The Reality: Standard deviation (σ, s) quantifies the dispersion of individual data points around their mean. Standard error (SE = σ / √n) quantifies the dispersion of sample means across repeated experiments, shrinking as sample size increases.
