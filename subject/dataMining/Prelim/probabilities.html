PART ONE: Basic Probabilities & Fundamentals

1. Why It Matters Decisions Are Made Under Uncertainty

Life is full of uncertain events, and we usually have to weigh the possible outcomes before we decide. Executives face the same problem at a much larger scale, whether they are funding a research project, approving a merger, or walking away from a buyout.

Probability and statistical data let them estimate how likely each outcome is, then make the call with evidence instead of instinct.

  • What is the chance of success?
  • What is the probability that we fail?
  • Is the risk actually worth taking?

2. Definition So what is probability?

  • 1. In plain words: The chance of something happening.
  • 2. More academically: The likelihood of an event occurring.

Same idea, two registers. Use the second one in your write-ups.

3. Terminology An event is an outcome, or a set of them

An event is a specific outcome, or a combination of several outcomes. The outcomes themselves can be almost anything:

  • A: Getting heads when flipping a coin
  • B: Rolling a four on a six sided die
  • C: Running a mile in under six minutes

One experiment can hold several events. A coin flip has two possible outcomes, so it has two events, and each one needs its own probability.

4. Notation We write probabilities as numbers

Knowing an event is merely likely or unlikely is rarely enough. We want to measure and compare, so we express probability numerically:

  • Percentage: 20%
  • Fraction: 1/5
  • Real number (conventional): 0.2

By convention we use real numbers between 0 and 1, so 0.2 rather than 20% or one fifth.

5. Interpretation Reading a probability value

ValueInterpretation
0absolutely will not happen
0.2unlikely
0.5even chance
0.66likely

Higher value, higher likelihood. Nothing more mysterious than that. Most events we care about land somewhere in the middle, so values like 0.2, 0.5 and 0.66 are what you should expect to see.

6. The Core Rule How we actually compute it

Formula
P(A) = number of preferred outcomes / total number of possible outcomes
• 1. Preferred outcomes: The results we want to see happen. Some textbooks call these favorable outcomes. Both terms mean the same thing.
• 2. Sample space: Every outcome that is possible at all. This is the denominator, and getting it right is usually the hard part.

7. Worked Examples Two straightforward cases & combined outcomes

  • Flipping a coin and getting heads: 1 preferred outcome / 2 possible outcomes → P(heads) = 1/2 = 0.5 (Heads is the only preferred outcome, and there are two possible outcomes in total).
  • Rolling a four on a six sided die: 1 preferred outcome / 6 possible outcomes → P(four) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167 (Still one preferred outcome, but the sample space is now three times larger).
  • Combined Outcomes (Rolling a number divisible by three): We would accept either a three or a six. Sample space stays at 6. Preferred outcomes = 2 (the faces 3 and 6). Possible outcomes = 6. Probability = 2/6 ≈ 0.33 (roughly a one in three chance).

8. A Useful Shortcut Independent events multiply

The probability of two independent events happening at the same time equals the product of their individual probabilities:

P(ace of spades) = P(ace) × P(spade) = (1/13) × (1/4) = 1/52

Treat independent events as events that do not affect each other.

9. Scale Check Winning the lottery

Preferred outcomes are the tickets you bought. Possible outcomes number upward of 175 million.

  • 0.0000000057: One ticket, roughly a 1 in 175,000,000 chance
  • 0.5: A single coin toss, for comparison

PART TWO: Expected Values & Experimental Probability

1. Foundations What is an experiment?

  • 1. Trial: One flip of the coin, with the outcome written down. That single action is a trial.
  • 2. Experiment: Many trials run together. Toss a coin 20 times and record all 20 outcomes, and that whole process is one experiment.

Example: One experiment, 20 trials (HTTHTHHTHTTHTHHHTHTH) → 11 heads out of 20 trials, so the experimental probability here is 0.55. Close to the theoretical 0.5, but not identical.

2. Two Kinds of Probability Where the number comes from

  • Theoretical (True Probability): Worked out from the structure of the problem itself, the way we did with coins, dice and cards.
  • Experimental Probability: Worked out by actually running the trials and counting what happened. No structural reasoning required.

The two are rarely identical, but the experimental value is a good approximation. We reach for it whenever the true probability is unknown, or would take far too many factors to compute.

3. The Experimental Rule

Experimental Formula
P = number of successful trials / total number of trials

Shop queue example: Eight out of ten visits to local shop waiting in line → 80% queue, 20% no queue.

4. Definition The Expected Value E(A)

The outcome we expect to occur when we run an experiment.

  • Categorical outcomes (Suits, colours, yes or no):
    E(A) = P(A) × n
    Example: Drawing a card 20 times with replacement for spades → E(spade) = 0.25 × 20 = 5. An expected value of 5 does not guarantee 5. You could draw 4 spades, or 6, or in principle all 20.
  • Numerical outcomes (Points, prices, quantities): Weight every value by its probability (Take each value in sample space, multiply by probability, add them all up).
    E(A) = ∑ (Value × Probability)
    Archery Target Example: Outer ring (10 pts, p=0.5 → 5), Second ring (20 pts, p=0.4 → 8), Bullseye (100 pts, p=0.1 → 10). Total E(A) = 5 + 8 + 10 = 23.

5. Why It Matters Predictions come as intervals

You can never score 23 with one shot. Expected values let us make predictions about the future based on past data. The future carries too much uncertainty for a single exact number, so we forecast a likely range instead (e.g., meteorologists: "3 to 5 feet of snow", "temperatures up to 90 degrees").

PART THREE: Probability Frequency Distribution

1. Two Dice Example Sum of Tops

Throwing two standard six-sided dice yields 36 possible outcomes (6 × 6). P(sum = 7) = 6 / 36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167. Expected value E(sum) = 7. Most probable is not the same as likely (seven carries a probability of only 1/6).

2. Definition & Expressions

Probability frequency distribution: A collection of the probabilities for each possible outcome. Expressed as a table or a graph.

3. Steps to Build a Probability Frequency Distribution

PART FOUR: Complements

1. Definition Everything the event is not

The complement of an event covers whatever the event leaves out, completing the rest of the sample space. Written as A'. Note that (A')' = A.

Example: Event A = Rolling an even number (2, 4, 6); Complement A' = Rolling an odd number (1, 3, 5).

2. The Rule Underneath It All outcomes total one

P(heads) + P(tails) = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1 (certainty). All probabilities in a sample space total one.

3. Diagnostics If your probabilities do not total one

  • Sum > 1: Double counting (some assumed outcomes occur at the same time).
  • Sum < 1: Missing outcomes from the sample space.

4. The Complement Formula

Formula
P(A') = 1 - P(A)

Reach for it when the event is crowded: count the few outcomes you do not want instead of many outcomes you do want.

Worked Example (Rolling 1, 2, 4, 5, or 6): Complement way is 1 - P(3) = 1 - 1/6 = 5/6. Same answer, one subtraction instead of five additions.

🎯 When to Use Each Formula & Practical Exam Examples

1. Permutations (Without Repetition)
n!

When to Use: Arranging ALL n items in an ordered sequence. Every single item is used once, and order is strictly critical.

Key Trigger Phrases: "arrange all items", "line up people in a row", "books on a shelf", "anagrams of distinct letters".

💡 Exam Example: In how many ways can you arrange 5 distinct books on a shelf?
Solution: n! = 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120 ways.
2. Variations (Without Repetition)
n! ÷ (n - p)!

When to Use: Selecting and ordering p items from a larger pool of n distinct items (p < n). Order matters and items cannot be reused.

Key Trigger Phrases: "1st, 2nd, and 3rd place", "President, VP, and Secretary", "running order for top finishers".

💡 Exam Example: 10 sprinters run in a race. In how many ways can Gold, Silver, and Bronze be awarded?
Solution: 10! ÷ (10 - 3)! = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720 podium finishes.
3. Variations (With Repetition)
np

When to Use: Filling p ordered positions where each position can independently pick from any of the n options (items can be repeated).

Key Trigger Phrases: "ATM PIN codes", "alphanumeric passwords", "coin toss sequences", "license plates".

💡 Exam Example: How many 4-digit PINs can be made using digits 0 to 9?
Solution: n = 10 digits, p = 4 slots → np = 104 = 10,000 PINs.
4. Combinations (Without Repetition)
n! ÷ [p! × (n - p)!]

When to Use: Selecting a subset of p items from n items where order does NOT matter and items cannot repeat.

Key Trigger Phrases: "lottery numbers", "poker hand of cards", "select a committee / team of members", "sample group".

💡 Exam Example: Choosing 6 numbers out of 49 in a standard lottery draw:
Solution: 49! ÷ [6! × 43!] = 13,983,816 combinations.
5. Combinations (With Repetition)
(n + p - 1)! ÷ [p! × (n - 1)!]

When to Use: Selecting p items from n distinct categories where order does NOT matter, but multiple items can belong to the same category.

Key Trigger Phrases: "buy p scoops of ice cream from n flavors", "choose p donuts from n types", "stars and bars".

💡 Exam Example: Buying 5 scoops of ice cream from 3 flavors (Vanilla, Chocolate, Strawberry):
Solution: n = 3, p = 5 → (3 + 5 - 1)! ÷ [5! × 2!] = 7! ÷ (120 × 2) = 21 combinations.

🏛️ Pillar 1: Core Terminology & Keyword Recall Grid

Probability & Sample Space

Probability P(E): A numerical measure of uncertainty bounded strictly between 0 and 1 (0% to 100%).
Sample Space (S): The exhaustive set of all possible elementary outcomes. Axiom: Σ P(xᵢ) = 1.0.

Classical vs Empirical

Classical (Theoretical): P(A) = Preferred ÷ Total. Assumes all elementary outcomes are equally likely.
Empirical (Experimental): P_exp(A) = Successes ÷ Trials. Derived from observed experimental frequencies.

Range Axiom & Events

Range Axiom: 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1.
Certain Event: P(A) = 1.0 (Guaranteed).
Impossible Event: P(A) = 0.0 (Cannot occur).
Complement: P(A') = 1 − P(A).

Independent vs Dependent

Independent: Occurrence of A does not alter probability of B: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).
Dependent: Occurrence of A changes B's chance: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A).

Mutually Exclusive vs Joint

Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint): Events cannot occur together: P(A ∩ B) = 0. P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).
Overlapping (Joint): Events can co-occur: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).

Expected Value E(X)

Categorical Variable: E(A) = n × P(A) (Expected frequency/count).
Numerical Variable: E(X) = Σ [xᵢ × P(xᵢ)] (Probability-weighted average / center of mass).

Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

As the number of independent trials approaches infinity (n → ∞), the experimental probability P_exp(A) converges mathematically to the theoretical probability P(A).

Permutations

Arranging ALL n distinct items in a specific sequential order without repetition: n! (Order strictly matters; every item is used once).

Variations

Selecting and arranging p items from a pool of n items (p < n) where order matters.
• Without repetition: n! ÷ (n − p)!
• With repetition: nᵖ

Combinations

Selecting an unordered subset of p items from n items where order does NOT matter.
• Without repetition: n! ÷ [p!(n − p)!]
• With repetition: (n + p − 1)! ÷ [p!(n − 1)!]

⚠️ Pillar 4: Common Exam Pitfalls & Fallacies

Pitfall 1: Mutually Exclusive vs Independent Trap

Exam Trap: Assuming two events that cannot occur together are "independent."
Correct Reality: If P(A) > 0 and P(B) > 0, mutually exclusive events CANNOT be independent. If A occurs, the probability of B instantly drops to zero (P(B|A) = 0 ≠ P(B)).

Pitfall 2: The Gambler's Fallacy

Exam Trap: Believing that after flipping 5 Heads in a row, the 6th flip is "due" to be Tails.
Correct Reality: Each coin flip is an independent trial. The coin has no memory: P(Tails on 6th flip) remains exactly 0.50 (50%).

Pitfall 3: Forgetting Overlap Subtraction

Exam Trap: Calculating P(Ace ∪ Spade) as 4/52 + 13/52 = 17/52.
Correct Reality: The Ace of Spades is contained in BOTH sets. You MUST subtract the intersection: 4/52 + 13/52 − 1/52 = 16/52 = 4/13.

Pitfall 4: Expected Value Realizability

Exam Trap: Expecting E(X) to be an integer or an actual possible single outcome.
Correct Reality: Rolling a 6-sided die yields E(X) = 3.5. You will never roll a 3.5 on a single die; E(X) is the long-term mathematical average across infinite rolls.

Pitfall 5: Variations vs Combinations Order Rule

Exam Trap: Using combinations when assigning ranked positions (Gold, Silver, Bronze).
Correct Reality: Order Matters → Variation / Permutation. Order Does Not Matter → Combination.
