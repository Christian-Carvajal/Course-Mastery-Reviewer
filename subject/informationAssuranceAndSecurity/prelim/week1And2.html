PART 1: Foundations of Information Security & The CIA Triad

1.1 What is Information Security?

Information security is the practice of protecting information and information systems from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction. ISO/IEC 27001 defines it compactly as the preservation of confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information.

Three Core Principles of InfoSec
  • A Process, Not a Product: Security is an ongoing cycle of risk assessment, control implementation, and monitoring rather than a piece of software you buy once.
  • A Business Function: InfoSec enables business operations safely rather than serving as a hurdle or bottleneck to progress.
  • Shared Responsibility: Technology provides controls, but human choices, policies, and behavior determine actual security posture.

1.2 Information Security vs Cybersecurity vs Information Assurance

While often used interchangeably, these terms cover distinct scopes:

  • Information Security (InfoSec): The overarching discipline protecting information in all forms—physical paper files, digital databases, spoken conversations, and hardware assets.
  • Cybersecurity: A specialized sub-discipline focused strictly on protecting digital assets, networks, cloud systems, and internet-connected infrastructure from digital threats.
  • Information Assurance (IA): The strategic management umbrella that ensures data is correct, available when needed, authentic, non-repudiated, and backed by robust risk management and business continuity plans.

1.3 The CIA Triad

The Fundamental Framework
CONFIDENTIALITY • INTEGRITY • AVAILABILITY
Every security control, policy, or incident response procedure is designed to protect one or more pillars of the CIA Triad.
1. Confidentiality (Prevent Unauthorized Disclosure)

Ensuring that sensitive information is accessible only to authorized individuals, entities, or processes.

  • Key Threats: Eavesdropping, shoulder surfing, packet sniffing, unauthorized file access, data exfiltration, social engineering.
  • Core Controls: AES encryption (at rest and in transit), Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA), Data Loss Prevention (DLP) tools, non-disclosure agreements.
  • Real-World Breach Example: An unencrypted database containing customer credit card numbers is exposed to the public internet.
2. Integrity (Prevent Unauthorized Modification)

Safeguarding the accuracy, completeness, and trustworthiness of data and systems throughout their entire lifecycle.

  • Key Threats: Unauthorized data tampering, man-in-the-middle (MitM) alterations, malware payload injection, unauthorized SQL updates, transmission corruption.
  • Core Controls: Cryptographic hashes (SHA-256), digital signatures, file integrity monitoring (FIM), database transaction logging, write-once backups.
  • Real-World Breach Example: An attacker alters bank account balance records or tampers with medical dosage records in a hospital database.
3. Availability (Prevent Unauthorized Disruption)

Ensuring that authorized users have timely and reliable access to information, systems, and critical services when needed.

  • Key Threats: Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks, ransomware encryption of production systems, hardware failures, power outages, physical disasters.
  • Core Controls: Redundant server clusters, load balancers, automated failover systems, RAID storage arrays, offsite backups, Disaster Recovery Plans (DRP).
  • Real-World Breach Example: A SYN-flood DDoS attack knocks out an e-commerce storefront during peak holiday shopping hours.

1.4 Extending the Triad: The Parkerian Hexad

Donn Parker expanded the traditional CIA Triad to 6 security elements to address broader security requirements:

  1. Confidentiality: Secrecy and restricted access.
  2. Integrity: Prevention of unauthorized data modification.
  3. Availability: Timely access to systems and services.
  4. Authenticity: Verification of the genuine identity of a user, message, or system source.
  5. Possession / Control: Physical or logical control over data and physical media (e.g., a stolen encrypted laptop maintains confidentiality but loses physical possession).
  6. Utility: The usefulness or usability of data (e.g., data encrypted with a lost key retains confidentiality but loses utility).

PART 2: Threat Landscape, Vulnerabilities & Attack Vectors

2.1 Core Security Vocabulary: Asset, Threat, Vulnerability, Risk

Risk Formula
RISK = THREAT × VULNERABILITY × IMPACT
• Asset: Anything of value to the organization (data, infrastructure, IP, reputation, personnel).
• Threat: Any potential cause of an unwanted incident that may result in harm to a system or asset.
• Vulnerability: A flaw, weakness, or gap in security procedures, design, or implementation that can be exploited.
• Risk: The potential for loss, damage, or destruction resulting from a threat exploiting a vulnerability.
• Control (Safeguard): A measure put in place to mitigate vulnerability or reduce impact.

2.2 Threat Actor Categories

Threat actors vary widely in intent, resources, technical capability, and motivation:

  • Script Kiddies: Unskilled individuals who run pre-packaged automated scripts and exploits written by others, typically motivated by ego or curiosity.
  • Hacktivists: Individuals or groups motivated by political, ideological, or social causes (e.g., Anonymous, disabling government sites in protest).
  • Cybercriminals: Profit-driven individuals or organized syndicates conducting ransomware, financial fraud, identity theft, and extortion.
  • Insider Threats: Disgruntled employees, contractors, or careless staff members who misuse legitimate access to leak or destroy data.
  • Nation-State Actors (APTs): Highly sophisticated, well-funded state-backed threat groups (Advanced Persistent Threats) targeting critical infrastructure, intelligence, and intellectual property over long campaigns.

2.3 Common Attack Vectors

Attack Vector Taxonomy
  • Social Engineering & Phishing: Tricking human users into revealing credentials, executing malicious attachments, or transferring funds (Phishing, Spear Phishing, Whaling, Vishing, Smishing).
  • Malware & Ransomware: Malicious code (viruses, worms, trojans, ransomware, spyware, rootkits) designed to compromise or encrypt target systems.
  • Web Application Attacks: Exploiting application vulnerabilities like SQL Injection (SQLi), Cross-Site Scripting (XSS), Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF), and Remote Code Execution (RCE).
  • Network & Denial of Service Attacks: Disrupting service availability through Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) eavesdropping, ARP spoofing, DNS poisoning, and DDoS amplification.
  • Supply Chain Attacks: Compromising trusted third-party vendor software updates or hardware components to breach downstream targets.

📐 Pillar 2: Formula & Calculation Reference Cards

Exact mathematical equations, parameter definitions, step-by-step worked calculations, and plain English verbalizations.

ISO 27005 Composite Risk Equation
Risk Score = Threat Likelihood × Vulnerability Severity × Asset Criticality
Parameter Definitions:
  • Threat Likelihood (1–5): Probability and frequency of threat actor exploitation.
  • Vulnerability Severity (1–5): Ease of exploitability and exposure breadth.
  • Asset Criticality (1–5): Financial, operational, and reputational value of target asset.
Plain English Reading: "Risk is the product of how likely a threat will strike, how severe the system's weakness is, and how valuable the target asset is to the business."
Worked Example: Core DB (Asset = 5), Ransomware bot (Threat = 4), Unpatched RCE (Vuln = 4) → 5 × 4 × 4 = 80 / 125 (CRITICAL RISK → 24h SLA).
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Composite risk equals threat likelihood multiplied by vulnerability severity multiplied by asset criticality value."
Residual Risk Reduction Equation
Residual Risk = Inherent Risk - Impact of Safeguards
Parameter Definitions:
  • Inherent Risk: Raw risk exposure existing before any security controls are deployed.
  • Impact of Safeguards: Total risk reduction provided by administrative, technical, and physical controls.
  • Residual Risk: Net remaining risk that the organization must formally accept, transfer, or mitigate.
Plain English Reading: "The risk remaining after all security controls are installed equals the original unmitigated risk minus the protection provided by those controls."
Governance Rule: If Residual Risk ≤ Risk Tolerance, the risk can be accepted by the named Risk Owner.
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Residual risk is the remaining danger after subtracting the mitigating impact of applied security safeguards from inherent risk."

4. Web Application Attacks vs. Network Layer Attacks

Attack Vector Target / OSI Layer Exploitation Mechanism Primary Defensive Countermeasure
SQL Injection (SQLi) Application / DB (Layer 7) Injecting malicious SQL syntax into unsanitized user inputs to execute arbitrary DB queries. Parameterized Queries / Prepared Statements, ORM, Input Validation.
Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) Application / Client (Layer 7) Injecting malicious JavaScript executed inside a victim's browser session (Stored, Reflected, DOM). Context-aware Output Encoding, Content Security Policy (CSP), HttpOnly cookies.
Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF) Application / Auth (Layer 7) Tricking an authenticated browser into submitting unauthorized requests to a trusted site. Anti-CSRF synchronizer tokens, SameSite=Strict cookie attribute, Re-authentication.
Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) Network / Transport (Layers 2–4) Interception, reading, or tampering with active communication packets between two endpoints. TLS 1.3 encryption, Mutual TLS (mTLS), Certificate Pinning, VPNs.
ARP Spoofing / Poisoning Data Link (Layer 2) Sending forged ARP replies across local LAN to map the attacker's MAC to the default gateway's IP. Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI), Static ARP tables, 802.1X Port Security.
DNS Poisoning / Hijacking Network / Application (Layers 4–7) Injecting false DNS records into a caching resolver to redirect users to a malicious fake server. DNSSEC (DNS Security Extensions), DNS-over-HTTPS (DoH), Secure Resolvers.

⚠️ Pillar 4: Common Exam Pitfalls & Fallacies

Critical conceptual distinctions and instructor test traps that frequently cause lost exam points.

Pitfall 1: Possession vs. Utility (Hexad)

The Trap: Assuming a lost or encrypted laptop breaches confidentiality.
The Reality:

  • Stolen Encrypted Laptop: Maintains Confidentiality (ciphertext protected), but loses Possession / Control.
  • Ransomware Encrypted File (Lost Key): Maintains Confidentiality & Possession, but loses Utility (data is unreadable).

Pitfall 2: Tailgating vs. Piggybacking

The Trap: Treating tailgating and piggybacking as exact synonyms.
The Reality:

  • Tailgating: Attacker follows an authorized employee through a secure doorway without their consent or awareness.
  • Piggybacking: Authorized employee knowingly holds the door open for the unauthorized individual out of misplaced politeness.

Pitfall 3: Spear Phishing vs. Whaling

The Trap: Confusing the target profile of targeted phishing attacks.
The Reality:

  • Spear Phishing: Highly customized email targeting any specific individual or role (e.g., HR clerk, engineer).
  • Whaling: Specialized spear phishing targeting exclusively C-suite / high-profile executives (CEO, CFO, Board members).

Pitfall 4: Confidentiality vs. Integrity Breaches

The Trap: Misclassifying unauthorized reading as an integrity violation.
The Reality:

  • Unauthorized Reading / Snooping: Violates Confidentiality (data remains intact and unmodified).
  • Unauthorized Modifying / Overwriting: Violates Integrity (data accuracy and completeness is corrupted).

Pitfall 5: Vulnerability vs. Threat vs. Risk

The Trap: Stating "Ransomware is a vulnerability" or "A missing patch is a threat."
The Reality:

  • Missing Software Patch: The Vulnerability (internal system flaw).
  • Cybercrime Syndicate / Malware: The Threat (external danger/actor).
  • Expected Financial Downtime ($): The resulting Risk.

Pitfall 6: Security is a Process, Not a Product

The Trap: Believing purchasing an expensive firewall or EDR tool makes an organization "secure".
The Reality: Security is an ongoing cycle of risk assessment, policy enforcement, user training, and continuous monitoring. Technology is only one layer of a holistic defense-in-depth posture.
