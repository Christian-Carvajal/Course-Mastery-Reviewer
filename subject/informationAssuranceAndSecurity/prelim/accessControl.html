1. Why access control matters

In the first weeks of this course you learned that information security protects the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of data. Access control is the mechanism that actually enforces those goals in day-to-day operation. It is the set of rules and technologies that decide who is allowed to do what to which resource. Without it, a policy that says "only the registrar may change grades" is just a sentence on paper.

Think of a university. A student should see their own grades but not change them. A professor should change grades for their own classes but not for another department. The registrar can correct records across the whole institution. Access control is what turns those intentions into enforced, checkable rules. When it is done well, it is invisible. When it is done badly, it is how a curious clerk reads records they should never see, or how a single stolen password exposes an entire database.

2. The three steps: identification, authentication, authorization

Every access decision moves through three steps, in order. Students often blur them together, but they answer three different questions and use different mechanisms.

Step The question it answers Everyday example
Identification Who do you claim to be? Typing your username or student number.
Authentication Can you prove it? Entering your password, a one-time code, or scanning a fingerprint.
Authorization What are you allowed to do? The system checks that this account may open the grades file.
Key distinction to remember

Authentication proves who you are. Authorization decides what you may do. A valid login does not automatically grant access to everything—you can be correctly authenticated and still be denied, because authorization is a separate check.

2.1 Factors of authentication

Authentication relies on one or more factors. Combining factors from different categories—called multi-factor authentication (MFA)—is far stronger than any single one, because an attacker must defeat two unrelated things at once.

  • Something you know: a password, a PIN, an answer to a security question.
  • Something you have: a phone that receives a code, a hardware token, an ID card.
  • Something you are: a fingerprint, a face scan, or another biometric trait.

3. The Principle of Least Privilege

The Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP) states that every user, program, or process should be given only the access it needs to do its job, and no more. A teaching assistant who only records attendance does not need permission to edit final grades. A backup script that only reads files does not need permission to delete them.

Least privilege matters because it limits the blast radius when something goes wrong. If an account is compromised, or a user makes a mistake, the damage is capped at whatever that account was allowed to touch. An over-privileged account is a single point of catastrophic failure; a least-privileged one is a contained incident.

Least privilege in one sentence

Give each account the smallest set of permissions that still lets it do its legitimate work—then a stolen password or a careless click can only reach that small set, not everything.

A closely related idea is separation of duties: no single person should control an entire sensitive process from start to finish. The person who requests a payment should not also be the one who approves it. Together, least privilege and separation of duties make both mistakes and insider abuse much harder.

4. Access control models

An access control model is the overall strategy an organization uses to decide who gets access to what. The core three you must be able to compare are DAC, MAC, and RBAC; a fourth, ABAC, extends the idea and is increasingly common. They differ in one key question: who or what has the authority to grant access?

4.1 Discretionary Access Control (DAC)

In DAC, the owner of a resource decides who may access it. If you create a file, you can choose to share it, and with whom. This is the model behind file sharing on Windows, macOS, and Google Drive—the person who owns the document sets the permissions.

  • Strength: Flexible and easy for users; access can be granted quickly by whoever owns the resource.
  • Weakness: Inconsistent and hard to govern at scale. Owners can grant access carelessly, and permissions sprawl over time.
  • Best fit: Small teams and personal or collaborative file sharing where flexibility matters more than strict control.

4.2 Mandatory Access Control (MAC)

In MAC, a central authority sets access rules based on classification labels, and individual users cannot change them. Data is labelled (for example, Confidential, Secret, Top Secret) and users are given a clearance level. Access is granted only when a user's clearance meets the data's label. This is the model used by militaries and intelligence agencies.

  • Strength: Extremely strong and consistent; users cannot override the rules, so accidental oversharing is impossible.
  • Weakness: Rigid and administratively heavy; every user and resource must be labelled and maintained centrally.
  • Best fit: High-security environments—military, government, and anywhere data classification is legally required.

4.3 Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)

In RBAC, access is granted to roles rather than to individuals, and users are assigned to roles based on their job. A "Registrar" role can edit grades; anyone placed in that role inherits that permission. When a person changes jobs, you simply change their role rather than re-editing dozens of individual permissions. This is the model used by most companies, universities, and cloud platforms today.

  • Strength: Scales well and maps cleanly to how organizations actually work; onboarding and role changes are simple.
  • Weakness: Requires careful upfront design of roles; poorly designed roles lead to "role explosion" or over-broad access.
  • Best fit: Most medium-to-large organizations—the practical default for businesses and schools.

4.4 Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)

In ABAC, access is decided by attributes and rules rather than by ownership, labels, or a fixed role. An attribute is any fact about the user (department, clearance, job title), the resource (its classification, owner, type), or the context (time of day, location, device). Rules combine these into policies such as: "allow access if the user is in the Finance department AND the request is during office hours AND the device is company-managed." This is the model behind modern cloud platforms and zero-trust systems.

  • Strength: Extremely fine-grained and flexible; a single policy can express conditions that would need many separate roles in RBAC.
  • Weakness: Complex to design and to audit; with many attributes it can be hard to predict exactly who gets access, and mistakes are easy to hide.
  • Best fit: Large, dynamic environments—cloud services, zero-trust networks, and systems where access must depend on context, not just identity.
RBAC vs. ABAC in one line

RBAC asks "what is your role?" and grants access to that role. ABAC asks "what are the facts about you, the resource, and right now?" and evaluates a rule. ABAC can do everything RBAC does and more, at the cost of greater complexity—which is why many organizations start with RBAC and add ABAC rules only where they need context.

5. Worked example: two organizations, two choices

A university registrar system

Thousands of users fall into a handful of clear job types: students, professors, department heads, and registrar staff. Permissions should follow the job, not the person, and must stay consistent as people enrol, graduate, and change departments. RBAC fits: define roles once, assign people to them, and access stays correct as individuals come and go. DAC would be chaos at that scale; MAC's classification labels are more rigidity than a university needs.

A military intelligence unit

Data is classified by sensitivity, disclosure can be catastrophic, and no individual may be allowed to widen access on their own. Control matters far more than convenience. MAC fits: a central authority sets clearances and labels, and the system enforces them regardless of what any user wants. RBAC alone would not capture the strict clearance-versus-classification rule that this environment legally requires.

📐 Pillar 2: Formula & Calculation Reference Cards

Cryptographic entropy, brute-force keyspaces, and formal Multi-Level Security (MLS) mathematical rules.

Password Shannon Information Entropy
E = L × log2(R)
Parameter Definitions:
  • L: Password length in number of characters.
  • R: Character pool size (Digits: 10, Lowercase: 26, Alphanumeric: 62, Full ASCII: 94).
  • E: Total information entropy measured in bits.
Plain English Reading: "Entropy measures the bit-strength of a password by multiplying length by the binary logarithm of character possibilities per position."
Worked Example: L = 12, R = 94 → E = 12 × log2(94) = 12 × 6.5546 = 78.66 bits (>64-bit NIST benchmark).
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Password entropy in bits equals password length L multiplied by base-2 logarithm of character pool size R."
Brute Force Search Space & Crack Time
Keyspace = RL  |  Avg Crack Time = (RL ÷ 2) ÷ Hash Rate
Parameter Definitions:
  • RL: Total possible password combinations in the search space.
  • Hash Rate: Number of password hashes the attacker's GPU rig computes per second.
Plain English Reading: "On average, a brute-force attacker finds the password after searching 50% of the keyspace; crack time is that 50% divided by computational speed."
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Total keyspace equals character pool R raised to length L. Average crack time equals keyspace divided by twice the testing hash rate."
Bell-LaPadula (BLP) Confidentiality Formal Rules
Simple Security (ss): Read(S, O) ⇔ Clearance(S) ≥ Label(O)
*-Property (Star): Write(S, O) ⇔ Clearance(S) ≤ Label(O)
Core Rules:
  • No Read Up (Simple Security): A subject cannot read an object of higher classification (prevents unauthorized snooping).
  • No Write Down (*-Property): A subject cannot write to an object of lower classification (prevents declassifying secrets).
Plain English Reading: "You can only read at or below your clearance level, and you can only write at or above your clearance level."
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Bell-LaPadula Confidentiality: No Read Up (Simple Security) and No Write Down (*-Property)."
Biba Integrity Formal Rules
Simple Integrity: Read(S, O) ⇔ Integrity(S) ≤ Integrity(O)
*-Integrity: Write(S, O) ⇔ Integrity(S) ≥ Integrity(O)
Core Rules:
  • No Read Down (Simple Integrity): A high-integrity subject cannot read low-integrity, untrusted data (prevents corruption).
  • No Write Up (*-Integrity): A low-integrity subject cannot write or modify high-integrity files (prevents tampering).
Plain English Reading: "You can only read trusted data at or above your level, and you can only write data at or below your trust level."
🗣️ How to Read in English
Read as: "Biba Integrity: No Read Down (Simple Integrity) and No Write Up (*-Integrity)."

⚠️ Pillar 4: Common Exam Pitfalls & Fallacies

High-yield test traps on MLS properties, multi-factor rules, and authorization boundaries.

Pitfall 1: Authentication vs. Authorization

The Trap: Believing a valid login guarantees access to files.
The Reality:

  • Authentication: Strictly validates WHO you are.
  • Authorization: Strictly determines WHAT you can do. A successfully authenticated user can still be blocked by authorization checks.

Pitfall 2: Multi-Factor vs. Multi-Step Fallacy

The Trap: Entering a Password AND a PIN constitutes Two-Factor Authentication (2FA).
The Reality: Both a Password and a PIN belong to the same category ("Something You Know"). True MFA requires ≥2 different factor categories (e.g., Knowledge + Possession).

Pitfall 3: Bell-LaPadula *-Property Rationale

The Trap: Misunderstanding why "No Write Down" exists in Bell-LaPadula.
The Reality: "No Write Down" prevents a user with Top-Secret clearance (or trojan malware running under their credentials) from declassifying and copying Top-Secret intelligence into an Unclassified document.

Pitfall 4: Biba *-Property Rationale

The Trap: Inverting the write rule in Biba Integrity.
The Reality: "No Write Up" prevents an untrusted, unvetted lower-tier subject from modifying, writing to, or injecting malicious/corrupt data into critical high-integrity files.

Pitfall 5: Least Privilege vs. Need-to-Know

The Trap: Treating PoLP and Need-to-Know as identical concepts.
The Reality:

  • Least Privilege (PoLP): Restricts system-level action rights (e.g., read, write, execute, admin privileges).
  • Need-to-Know: Restricts access to specific sensitive data objects based on active business assignment.

Pitfall 6: Implicit Deny vs. Explicit Deny

The Trap: Thinking Implicit Deny can override an explicit Allow rule.
The Reality:

  • Implicit Deny: The default fallback state when no rules match.
  • Explicit Deny: An active override rule that supersedes any permit/allow rule regardless of evaluation order.
