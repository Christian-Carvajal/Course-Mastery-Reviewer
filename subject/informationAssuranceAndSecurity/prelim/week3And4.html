PART 1: Information Security Governance, Policies & ISO 27001

1.1 What is Information Security Governance?

Security governance is the system by which an organization directs, controls, and oversees its information security strategy. It ensures that security activities align with organizational goals, comply with laws, and manage risks responsibly.

Governance vs Management
  • Governance (Leadership & Direction): Executive leadership sets security objectives, defines risk appetite, allocates resources, and evaluates oversight.
  • Management (Execution & Operations): Security teams plan, build, run, and monitor security controls to achieve executive direction.

1.2 The Document Hierarchy: Policy, Standard, Guideline, Procedure

Organizations govern security through a structured hierarchy of four distinct document types:

1. Information Security Policy (Mandatory, High-Level)

Executive management's formal statement of intent, rules, and direction regarding security.

  • Characteristics: Concise, high-level, technology-neutral, mandatory across the organization.
  • Example: "All organizational data must be protected against unauthorized access, loss, or disclosure."
2. Standards (Mandatory, Specific)

Mandatory, measurable requirements and baseline rules supporting security policies.

  • Characteristics: Specific, measurable, enforceable, technology-focused.
  • Example: "User passwords must be at least 12 characters, include 3 character classes, and expire every 90 days."
3. Guidelines (Discretionary, Advisory)

Recommended best practices and advisory suggestions that assist with compliance.

  • Characteristics: Non-mandatory, flexible, advisory context.
  • Example: "It is recommended to use password manager software to generate random passphrases."
4. Procedures & Work Instructions (Mandatory, Step-by-Step)

Detailed step-by-step instructions describing exactly how to perform specific tasks or operational processes.

  • Characteristics: Highly detailed, step-by-step SOPs, operational focus.
  • Example: "Step 1: Open AWS IAM Console. Step 2: Click Create User. Step 3: Attach MFA policy..."

1.3 The ISO/IEC 27001 Framework

ISO/IEC 27001 is the international benchmark standard for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Information Security Management System (ISMS).

Key ISO 27001 Components
  • ISMS Clauses (4 to 10): Mandatory requirements covering leadership commitment, risk assessment, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, and continual improvement.
  • Annex A Controls (93 Controls in ISO 27001:2022): A comprehensive catalog of security controls organized into 4 themes: Organizational, People, Physical, and Technological.
  • Statement of Applicability (SoA): A mandatory document declaring which Annex A controls apply to the organization, which are excluded, and the technical justification for each decision.

PART 2: Risk Management & Decision-Making Frameworks

2.1 The Risk Management Cycle (ISO 27005)

Risk management is an ongoing, iterative process rather than a one-time audit checklist:

5-Step Risk Management Cycle
IDENTIFY → ANALYZE → EVALUATE → TREAT → MONITOR
1. Context & Identification: Catalog assets, threats, existing controls, and vulnerabilities.
2. Risk Analysis: Determine likelihood and potential impact to calculate risk magnitude.
3. Risk Evaluation: Compare calculated risk against organizational risk appetite and tolerance thresholds.
4. Risk Treatment: Select and implement treatment strategies (Avoid, Mitigate, Transfer, Accept).
5. Monitoring & Review: Continuously monitor residual risks, control effectiveness, and emerging threats.

2.2 The 4 Risk Treatment Options

Selecting Risk Response Strategies
  • 1. Risk Mitigation (Reduction): Implementing security controls (firewalls, encryption, MFA, patching) to reduce vulnerability or impact.
  • 2. Risk Avoidance: Eliminating the risk entirely by choosing not to engage in the risky activity or retiring a vulnerable system.
  • 3. Risk Transfer (Sharing): Shifting financial or operational impact to a third party (e.g., purchasing cybersecurity insurance or outsourcing to a managed vendor with SLA indemnification).
  • 4. Risk Acceptance: Formally deciding to carry the risk without additional controls when the risk falls within tolerance or mitigation costs exceed potential loss. Must be signed off by a named Risk Owner.

2.3 Inherent Risk vs Residual Risk

Risk Equation
RESIDUAL RISK = INHERENT RISK - CONTROLS
• Inherent Risk: The raw level of risk present before any security controls or safeguards are applied.
• Residual Risk: The remaining risk that exists after security controls have been implemented.
• Goal: Reduce Residual Risk until it is equal to or below the organization's Risk Tolerance threshold.

2.4 Risk Matrix & Qualitative vs Quantitative Analysis

  • Qualitative Risk Analysis: Rates likelihood and impact using subjective scales (High, Medium, Low) on a Risk Matrix grid. Fast and intuitive for prioritizing risks.
  • Quantitative Risk Analysis: Assigns numerical monetary values to risk components using financial metrics:
    • Single Loss Expectancy (SLE): Asset Value (×) Exposure Factor.
    • Annualized Rate of Occurrence (ARO): Estimated frequency of occurrence per year.
    • Annualized Loss Expectancy (ALE): SLE (×) ARO.

Quick Reference: Quantitative Risk Analysis Formulas

Single Loss Expectancy
SLE = Asset Value (AV) × Exposure Factor (EF)
Monetary loss incurred each time a single threat event takes place.
Annualized Loss Expectancy
ALE = SLE × Annualized Rate of Occurrence (ARO)
Total estimated financial loss expected per year for a specific risk.
Qualitative Risk Rank
Risk Exposure = Likelihood × Impact
Position on a Risk Matrix grid (Red=Critical, Yellow=Moderate, Green=Low).

Quick Reference: BCP/DRP Metrics & Regulatory Compliance Framework

RTO vs RPO

RTO (Recovery Time Objective): Max acceptable downtime after disruption.
RPO (Recovery Point Objective): Max acceptable data loss volume measured in time.
